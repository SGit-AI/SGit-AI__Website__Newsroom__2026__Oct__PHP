What works

  • The thirty-second answer in the board briefing is exactly the shape I need: what happened, what agencies say, no confirmed break-in, and the three questions to ask. (briefings/board/index.html)
  • 'What to decide or do' is a ready-made list of board questions (inventory including vendor products, patched and restarted, who can pull a system, what a compromise would reach, evidence we were not hit). I can copy these straight into my notes. (briefings/board/index.html)
  • The 'At a glance' tiles (Is there a fix? Yes. Anyone confirmed hacked? Not yet.) tell me in seconds how worried to be. (index.html)
  • The opening paragraph is plain and reassuring in the right way: it says what PHP is, that a fix exists and that phones and laptops are not affected. (index.html)
  • The kitchen analogy and the 'What that can reach' list finally made the risk concrete for me: database passwords and other systems that trust the server. (explained/index.html)
  • The 'IN PLAIN ENGLISH' line at the top of the impact page saves a non-technical reader: two lists, many offline as a precaution, nobody confirmed broken into. (impact/index.html)
  • The Questions page answers 'Should we take our site offline?' and 'We are behind Cloudflare. Are we safe?' in short pasteable paragraphs, which are both questions I expect to hear in the boardroom. (questions/index.html)
  • The status words (confirmed, reported, unverified) and the sources under each fact make me trust that I am not repeating a rumour to the board. (index.html)

What this reader would change, in order

  1. Move the reading tracker, £ meter and Research box below the article on phones (or collapse them into one line), so the thirty-second answer is the first thing on the first screen. (briefings/board/index.html, medium)
    Between meetings I have seconds; today I scroll past controls I never use before I learn whether to worry.
  2. Add two or three sentences on cost: what downtime has looked like for organisations so far (for example Weird Gloop's 13 hours) and what a breach would put at risk, with a link to the CFO briefing. (briefings/board/index.html, small)
    The headline asks 'what is it costing us' and the page never answers it.
  3. Add one line for organisations that sell online: shop platforms such as Magento and WordPress shops run on PHP, and a compromise could reach customer data and payment settings. (briefings/board/index.html, small)
    It would tell me in one sentence that my company is squarely in scope and why it matters to us.
  4. Rewrite 'What is still open' for a director: keep 'nobody confirmed hacked yet' and 'the fix may not be complete', drop the video and Fediverse details or move them to the journalist briefing. (briefings/board/index.html, small)
    The two open points that change my board questions are buried among forensics I cannot judge.
  5. On phones, turn the two impact tables into stacked cards (who, what happened, who says so, status) instead of six narrow columns. (impact/index.html, medium)
    The page is the evidence that offline is a precaution, but on my phone it is unreadable slivers.
  6. Replace the mod_php and x86_64 sentence at the end of the impact page with a plain version: nobody yet knows how many unpatched sites were actually broken into. (impact/index.html, small)
    It is the conclusion of the page and I could not follow it.
  7. Add a 'What to ask management' heading wording (instead of 'What to decide or do') and number the questions. (briefings/board/index.html, small)
    Numbered questions are easier to read out in a meeting and to tick off as management answers.
  8. Put the questions first and move the librarian paragraph and the page catalogue to the bottom or another page. (questions/index.html, small)
    The useful short answers are hidden behind an introduction about how the site is run.

Lines this reader reacted to

  • briefings/board/index.html: Your questions are: do we know every system running PHP, are they updated and restarted, and who can pull a system offline and how fast.
    This is what I came for. I could read this sentence aloud at the board meeting.
  • briefings/board/index.html: What is our evidence that we were not exploited between the leak on 20 October and our patch?
    A good, pointed question I would not have thought to ask on my own.
  • briefings/board/index.html: Whether the deleted Calif video is the one now embedded on Calif's own write-up. Calif's page does not mention the leak.
    I have no idea who Calif is or why this matters to my company; it lost me.
  • index.html: Phones and personal computers are not affected.
    Useful straight away: it tells me this is about our servers, not our staff laptops.
  • explained/index.html: it does not matter how well a website is written; if PHP underneath it is unpatched, it is in scope.
    Clear and alarming in the right way: I cannot be told 'our code is fine' as an answer.
  • explained/index.html: Taking a site offline is a decision an organisation makes, not evidence it was attacked.
    Reassuring and worth repeating to colleagues who will see the headlines.
  • impact/index.html: the reported cost of php2shell so far is the cost of precaution.
    The single best line for a board: so far the damage is downtime, not breaches.
  • impact/index.html: how many were mod_php on x86_64 with default limits, reachable without a WAF, and how many of those show a worker spawning a shell.
    Complete jargon. I gave up on this paragraph.
  • questions/index.html: Safer, not safe.
    Three words I will use if someone tells me our firewall provider has it covered.

PHP October 2026 (php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai) tracks PHP news for one month. This month that is one story, the two critical PHP security flaws, followed as evidence: every claim with a status and its sources, every source captured, dated and hashed into a private vault. Built and run by one editor and a team of software agents; the roles and the method are public, and the site is rebuilt from the vault, never written by hand. One of the SGit Newsroom's story sites: up from here are October 2026, the year and the newsroom, whose own section on the story is here. Part of the sgit.ai network.
