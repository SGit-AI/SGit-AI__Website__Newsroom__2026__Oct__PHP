The story as a graph

In plain English A picture of which statements rest on which sources. Thick bundles mean well supported.

209 nodes and 281 edges: 70 claims, 68 events and the 71 sources they cite. There is no second graph behind this one: the page, the briefings and the timeline are all drawn from the same rows, so the picture cannot disagree with the text. The JSON is data/graph.json; this page shows the claims-to-sources slice.

How to read it

  • A claim with many lines to primary sources is well-founded; a claim with one line to a news page is one party's word relayed once.
  • The BBC live page sits near the top of the right column because the early claims all came through it; the work of the last day was to draw second lines from those claims to the pages the BBC was reporting on.
  • A source with no line is captured but not yet cited: a home page kept for context, a block recorded for the record, or a page whose claims nobody has traced yet. The sources page lists them all.
  • Events cite sources the same way; they are in the JSON and on the timeline, and are left off the drawing to keep it legible.

PHP October 2026 (php.oct.2026.newsroom.sgit.ai) tracks PHP news for one month. This month that is one story, the two critical PHP security flaws, followed as evidence: every claim with a status and its sources, every source captured, dated and hashed into a private vault. Built and run by one editor and a team of software agents; the roles and the method are public, and the site is rebuilt from the vault, never written by hand. One of the SGit Newsroom's story sites: up from here are October 2026, the year and the newsroom, whose own section on the story is here. Part of the sgit.ai network.
