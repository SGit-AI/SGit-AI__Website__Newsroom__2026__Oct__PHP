- The vulnerabilities are estimated to affect nearly 70% of websites.
- The vulnerabilities were independently discovered by two parties: Ryan Austin, a researcher at the California-based security firm Calif, as part of the OpenAI "Patch the Planet" project in August 2026, and the Israeli–American cloud security company Wiz, a subsidiary of Alphabet Inc. and a part of Google Cloud.

- PHP versions as early as 5.4 (released in 2012) were affected by these vulnerabilities, and while concerns around memory safety bugs had been raised decades before, no action was taken at the time because it was not thought that developing a working exploit could be feasible.

- Source code patches were released for version 7.4, 8.0, and 8.1, but no official updated binaries for those versions were released as they are considered end of life.
- Temporary mitigations were also published, but the maintainers said there were no complete mitigations and that updating was the only safe choice.
- Both vulnerabilities were published through GitHub security advisories on 21 October 2026 at 13:34 UTC, 26 minutes before the announced release time, with the PHP website updated shortly thereafter. cPanel and CloudLinux released backports in their ecosystems.
- Volunteer maintainer Ondřej Surý, who was given advance access to the fixes by the PHP project, prepared installable packages for old versions from the patched source code so that server administrators could automate deployments without compiling PHP themselves.
- In response to claims on X that unpatched older PHP releases left one-third of the internet permanently vulnerable, Surý stated that there were ways to patch and update the older versions as well.
- The PHP maintainers announced the update early and before release, after news of the vulnerabilities leaked on 20 October on the Hacker News forums, in a post on X (formerly Twitter) by cybersecurity company watchTowr labs, and in a proof-of-concept exploit video mistakenly released by Calif.
- The early notice on PHP.net encouraged users to prepare and update as soon as the patch was released.
- One security firm, Sansec, expected "mass attacks to start within 15 minutes of the release".
- Proof-of-concept exploits, write-ups, and partial mitigations started circulating on the Internet before the end of the embargo, after development platform Chainguard released patched versions of the software early; this allowed attackers to determine what code had been changed.
- Amazon Threat Intelligence identified a PHP2Shell exploitation attempt dating back to 17 October 2026, in MadPot telemetry, four days before public disclosure.
- Cloudflare reported signs of early exploitation prior to public patch release, and deployed temporary web application firewall rules as an attempted stopgap mitigation against the vulnerability.
- Security firm watchTowr said it had observed exploitation in the wild by 21 October through its honeypot network, while the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency said it had not observed exploitation as of the same day.
- The potential impacts of the vulnerability were widespread.
- Approximately 69% of websites whose server-side programming language is known by W3Techs use PHP, including those that use content management systems such as Drupal, Joomla, and WordPress. PHP is also bundled with vendor appliances, web host control panels such as cPanel and Plesk, and some Windows applications.

- Some Canadian government websites were temporarily taken down as a precaution as taken following guidance from the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security.
- The US National Security Agency recommended that websites go offline if unable to update immediately.
- The NCSC released a similar warning, urging organizations to be wary of backdoors.
- The Japanese National Cybersecurity Office advised PHP users to immediately update their systems.
- The Singaporean Cyber Security Agency has also urged organizations in Singapore to fix the security flaws.
- The Wikimedia Foundation shut down many of their supporting services, and forum sites such as 4chan and Something Awful went offline.
- Several Japanese websites and applications also went offline, including games such as Umamusume: Pretty Derby, Granblue Fantasy, Princess Connect! Re:Dive, and numerous e-commerce sites.
- The Internet Archive briefly shut down to fix the issue.
- Researchers at Wiz stated that the issue was on par with Heartbleed, a 2014 vulnerability that affected OpenSSL, an encryption library used by about two-thirds of the web at the time.
- Commentary in The Guardian noted that the PHP Foundation, which maintains the PHP language, only had 13 full- and part-time employees, and relied on financial contributions, whereas AI research, which was credited with helping discover the vulnerability, received "billions" in investment.
